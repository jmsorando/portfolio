Accuracy & agreement of the ORB Sport mouthguard heart-rate during laboratory and Muay Thai: a pilot study

Jose Miguel Sorando Campillo & Thomas Watkinson · UCL · Institute of Sport, Exercise & Health · ORB

Introduction

Wearable heart rate (HR) monitors using photoplethysmography (PPG) are widely used to guide training intensity and monitor athlete workload [1]. While wrist-worn PPG devices show acceptable accuracy at rest (MAE ~2.15 bpm), errors increase substantially during exercise (MAE ~7.7–10.6 bpm) due to motion artefacts [1,2]. Accuracy degrades further during high-intensity, variable activities such as trail running (MAPE >10%), where PPG-based devices consistently fail to meet acceptable validity thresholds [3]. Motion artefact remains the primary source of PPG signal degradation during exercise [4,5]. In contact sports, traditional wrist and chest-worn devices are impractical due to rules, safety concerns, and equipment interference.

Mouthguard-embedded PPG sensors offer a novel alternative by measuring HR from the oral cavity, where gingival blood vessels provide a stable vascular bed with low displacement during head impacts (<1 mm) [6]. However, validation data for oral PPG in real-world combat sports settings remains limited, particularly during high-impact activities such as sparring where jaw clenching, facial impacts, and rapid head movements may introduce unique signal artefacts.

Aims

Understand ORB Sport MG HR accuracy against ECG under:

  1. Controlled laboratory conditions
  2. Real-world Muay Thai training
  3. Specific activity types to identify which tasks degrade accuracy

Methods

Field session — Muay Thai (diagram description)

  • Bag work: guided combinations, metronome; RPE 3 → 9.
  • Structured sparring: 5 × 3 min, clinch, kickboxing and punches.
  • Free sparring: 3 × 3 min, participant-led intensity.

Laboratory session (diagram description)

  • Energy expenditure (EE): 4 × 5 min stages, 45 → 85% HRmax; 6 min steady-state incremental test.
  • VO₂max: incremental exercise to exhaustion.
  • HIIT: 3 × 1 min at 90% VO₂max.

Results

Agreement between the smart mouthguard and ECG varied by activity. Validity was strongest during controlled exercise tasks, with excellent agreement during EE and good agreement during VO₂. Agreement decreased during intermittent and combat-specific activities, with moderate agreement during intervals and bag work and poor agreement during structured and free sparring. The highest MAE values were observed during sparring, indicating reduced HR accuracy under high-contact, unpredictable movement conditions.

Bland–Altman plots show agreement between the smart mouthguard and ECG reference across the range of measured heart rates. In laboratory conditions, the mouthguard showed a small negative bias (−4.01 bpm) with relatively narrower limits of agreement (−27.93 to 19.92 bpm), indicating closer agreement with ECG under controlled testing conditions. In field conditions, bias became more negative (−15.25 bpm), and limits of agreement widened substantially (−67.80 to 37.30 bpm), indicating greater underestimation and higher variability in HR error. Overall, agreement was poorer in the field, suggesting that real-world combat-sport demands increased measurement error.

Interpretation

The ORB Sport mouthguard demonstrated stronger HR validity in controlled laboratory conditions than during real-world Muay Thai activity. Agreement with ECG was highest during controlled exercise tasks, particularly EE and VO₂ testing, where ICC values indicated excellent-to-good agreement. This suggests that mouthguard-embedded PPG can estimate HR reasonably well when movement patterns are more stable and sensor disruption is limited.

However, accuracy declined as activity became more combat-specific and unpredictable. The largest errors occurred during structured sparring and free sparring, where MAE increased to approximately 24–25 bpm and agreement was poor. Condition-effect analysis confirmed that only sparring produced significant increases in MAE, indicating that high-contact activity had the greatest impact on measurement accuracy.

The lab-field comparison further supports this pattern. Field testing significantly increased MAE, RMSE, and negative bias, showing that the mouthguard produced larger and more variable HR errors outside the laboratory. Bland–Altman analysis also showed wider limits of agreement in the field, suggesting poorer consistency and greater underestimation of HR during real-world training.

Overall, these findings suggest that the ORB Sport mouthguard may be suitable for controlled or lower-contact HR monitoring, but its accuracy is reduced during sparring. This is likely due to combat-specific sources of artefact, including jaw clenching, facial impacts, rapid head movement, and mouthguard displacement. Further algorithm refinement and sport-specific validation are needed before the device can be considered interchangeable with ECG during high-impact Muay Thai activity.

Future Considerations

  • Sport Diversity
  • Participant Diversity
  • Sparring Artifact
  • Algorithm Refinement
  • Sample Size

Conclusion

The ORB Sport mouthguard showed promising HR accuracy during controlled laboratory exercise, particularly during steady-state and VO₂-based testing. However, accuracy declined in field-based Muay Thai activity, with significantly greater error and poorer agreement during structured and free sparring. These findings suggest that mouthguard-embedded PPG may be suitable for lower-contact or controlled monitoring, but further algorithm refinement and sport-specific validation are needed before it can reliably replace ECG-based HR monitoring in high-impact combat sport settings.
