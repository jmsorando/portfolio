Predicting postprandial glucose and interpreting feature behaviour in free-living night shift workers

José M. Sorando Campillo · UCL · Institute of Sport, Exercise & Health

Introduction

Postprandial glucose (PPG) responses vary widely between individuals and predict long-term cardiometabolic risk (1,2). Machine learning (ML) approaches have been previously explored using sleep, activity, dietary and metabolic features to predict PPG, enabling personalised nutrition (3).

Feature analysis is a useful method used to reveal which inputs drive prediction. In a free-living digital cohort of over 1,000 day-active participants, Singh et al. found that glycaemic and temporally resolved dietary features were sufficient for accurate PPG prediction (4).

However, these models developed exclusively in ‘circadianly’ aligned cohorts and may not transfer to night shift workers, a group at elevated risk of dysglycaemia who eat and sleep out of phase with their circadian system (5).

Yet, whether existing prediction approaches generalise to this population, and whether the same features dominate when circadian alignment breaks down, is unknown.

Research Question

Can an XGBoost model accurately predict 2h postprandial glucose iAUC in night shift workers, and do the features driving prediction match those reported in day-active cohorts?

Aim(s)

  1. To evaluate the predictive performance of an XGBoost model for 2h postprandial glucose iAUC in a free-living night shift cohort.
  2. To characterise feature behaviour using SHAP values, identifying which features drive prediction.

Discussion

The XGBoost model including all features predicted 2h postprandial iAUC with modest but interpretable performance (R² = 0.236; MAE = 42.5 mmol·min/L; Figure 2), indicating glucose iAUC is partially predictable in free-living night-shift conditions, with substantial unexplained variability.

Feature ablation revealed that glycaemic features alone (12 features) achieved R² = 0.204, within 0.04 of the full 155-feature model (Figure 1). Adding diet composition and temporal diet features produced the largest incremental gains, while personal and sleep features contributed marginally. This parallels Singh et al.'s finding that glycaemic and temporally resolved dietary features are sufficient for accurate PPG prediction in day-active cohorts (4).

SHAP analysis showed prediction was driven mainly by baseline glycaemic state and recent dietary exposure, with baseline glucose, mean 24h glucose, CHO intake, and past 3h CHO among the strongest contributors (Figure 3). CHO intake showed a threshold-like positive contribution (Figure 4a), while baseline glucose showed a strong monotonic negative effect (Figure 4b), consistent with the incremental nature of iAUC and saturation in postprandial physiology, indicating the model captured meaningful biology. Past 3h CHO showed a non-linear positive effect (Figure 4c), suggesting recent carbohydrate exposure influenced iAUC beyond the index meal. Sleep fragmentation showed an inverse SHAP relationship (Figure 4d), but its marginal contribution in ablation suggests sleep effects may be partly captured by glycaemic and dietary context or are too subtle for this cohort size to resolve reliably.

Compared with larger PPGR studies (Zeevi 2015: R² ≈ 0.46, n = 800; Berry 2020: R² ≈ 0.59, n = 1,002), performance was lower, likely reflecting the smaller dataset (n = 69), absence of standardised test meals, and greater behavioural variability in free-living night-shift work. The feature hierarchy, however, mirrors that established in day-active populations, suggesting the core determinants of postprandial glucose are largely preserved under circadian misalignment (1,3).

Limitations & Future Considerations

  • Cohort size and ‘overfitting’
  • Scalability
  • Unexplored behavioural/physiological features
  • Self-reported dietary intake & Free-living conditions
  • Dimensionality and Feature redundancy

Conclusion

Overall, the XGBoost model predicted 2h postprandial glucose iAUC in free-living night shift workers, driven primarily by baseline glycaemic state and recent carbohydrate exposure. Although the feature hierarchy mirrors that established in day-active cohorts, suggesting the core determinants of postprandial glucose are preserved under circadian misalignment, sleep feature influence falls under top 20 most influential features, indicating relevance in PPGR influence. Future personalised nutrition tools for shift workers may therefore build on existing prediction frameworks, with larger cohorts needed to detect subtler shift-specific effects.
