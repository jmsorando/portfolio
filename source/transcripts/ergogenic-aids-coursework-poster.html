Ergogenic Potential of Tart Cherry in 1km Running Sprint: Biomarkers and Performance Outcomes

Introduction

Montmorency tart cherry (MTC) is a wild-cultivated tree native to parts of Europe and Asia. Over the last decade, MTC has received increasing research attention because its rich polyphenol content has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties that may reduce exercise-induced metabolic stress and central and peripheral fatigue (1–3). Acute pre-exercise dosing (commonly 1–3 h before activity) has been linked to reduced post-exercise soreness, although effects on short-distance performance and short-term recovery markers (Blood glucose, lactate) are inconsistent (4). The aim of this study was to compare 1 km running performance, perceived effort, and metabolic responses in recreationally active participants following an acute dose of MTC concentrate versus a taste-matched placebo to identify the possible benefits of MTC in sport performance.

Methods

Participant characteristics. Thirteen recreationally active adults (20–23 years) were asked to participate in a randomized, double-blind crossover trial. Participants gave informed consent and completed medical screening. Height and weight were measured (UKFloor stadiometer, Tanita scale).

Protocol. Participants consumed 30 mL Montmorency tart cherry concentrate diluted in 100 mL water or a taste-matched placebo 2 hours before testing. After a familiarization session 1 week prior and warm-up (self-determined dynamic/static stretching and 2-3min walk on the treadmill) before beginning the activity, participants performed a self-paced maximal 1 km treadmill time trial (hp cosmos pluto) separated by 1 week within each session. Heart rate was recorded continuously with Polar F10 monitors and logged at 250 m, 500 m, 750 m and 1 km; time of completion (seconds) and Borg RPE was recorded at the same checkpoints and at finish. Capillary finger-prick blood for lactate and glucose was taken pre-trial and immediately post-trial and analysed using the Biosen C-Line Sport (< 3% Coefficient of Variation).

*During week 2, external motivation by peer participants had a higher intensity and is reported here for transparency.

Statistical analysis. Primary outcomes were 1 km time, perceived exertion, and post-exercise lactate and glucose. A 2-way Repeated-measures ANOVA (Condition X Distance) was used with Greenhouse–Geisser correction if sphericity was violated; paired t-tests compared post-exercise biomarkers. Results are reported as mean ± SD with test statistics, p-values at 95% CIs.

Results

From the thirteen participants in the study, three were excluded from inferential analyses (two lost to follow-up; one extreme lactate outlier identified in the boxplot, Figure 4). Reported tests therefore use the remaining six participants.

A 2 (Condition: Cherry, Placebo) by 2 (Time: Pre, Post) repeated-measures ANOVA on blood lactate showed a non-significant Condition X Time interaction (F(1,8)=5.58, p = 0.056) (Table 3). Follow-up post-hoc paired comparisons indicated post-exercise lactate differed between conditions (post only: p = 0.044) while baseline lactate did not (p = 0.23) (Table 3). Blood glucose increased pre to post in both conditions but showed no significant Condition X Time interaction (ANOVA p = 0.341); the post-only comparison approached significance (p = 0.062) (Table 3).

A 2 by 4 repeated-measures ANOVA (Condition X Distance) found no significant condition effects for heart rate, completion time, or RPE (HR p=0.80, Time p=0.50, RPE p=0.37) (Table 3). Metabolic and perceptive responses showed very strong positive linear correlations: heart rate increased and started to plateau after 750m (Cherry R² = 0.974; Placebo R² = 0.916), completion time showed a near-linear relation with distance (Cherry R² = 0.943; Placebo R² = 0.925), and perceived exertion scaled as distance increased (Cherry R² = 0.987; Placebo R² = 0.979) (Figures 1–3). Individual plots show consistent pre-to-post rises in lactate and glucose and minimal separation by condition for HR, time and RPE (Figures 1–4); exercise produced the expected physiological increases, with a significantly larger post-trial lactate response in one condition (paired contrast). Results are reported as means ± SD (Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

There was no evidence that which suggested that acute MTC supplementation significantly alter HR, time, or RPE, but prior research supports its metabolic and recovery effects. Even though the visual trends (Figures 1–3) showed HR, completion time, and RPE all increased linearly with distance, they contained minimal separation between the lines (e.g., R² > 0.91 in all cases). This trend correlated with the non-significant p-values (p>0.05) suggesting that MTC supplement did not alter the performance of the 1km run. This study’s null findings contrast with some published evidence showing improved sprint/endurance performance during 15km bike trials or 60s sprint bike trial and recovery benefits from Montmorency cherry products, which might be explained by their different protocols or dosing strategies (4,5).

Concurrently, Levers et al. found no significant improvements in endurance performance following short-term powdered cherry supplementation, though they did observe reductions in markers of inflammation and oxidative stress (6). This supports the interpretation that MTC effects may be more recovery-related than directly ergogenic in sprint, checkpointed efforts (6). Furthermore, Figure 4 shows a separation in median post-exercise blood lactate between Placebo and MTC conditions. A post-hoc paired t-test on the post-exercise values reached significance, indicating lower lactate with MTC when the focused comparison is made outside the ANOVA family-wise test.

This can be related to the MTC recovery and ergogenic mechanism which is thought to aid recovery through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant polyphenols, especially flavonoids and anthocyanins. These compounds reduce exercise-induced oxidative stress and reduce pro-inflammatory signalling (e.g., lower cytokine responses and markers of muscle damage), which speed the decrease in muscle soreness and physiological recovery after strenuous exercise (1,3). Possible mechanisms include, reduction of reactive oxygen species (NADPH oxidases, or xanthine oxidases), and reduced inflammatory mediators (PI3K, TLR, JNK, etc) hence decreasing muscle damage and promoting faster recovery (6,7). This aligns with prior studies such as Mileva et al. and Connolly et al. that demonstrate that cherry juice can attenuate muscle damage and soreness following intensive strength exercise, likely via its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (1,3). Gao & Chilibeck similarly report that polyphenol-rich tart cherry products may improve post-exercise lactate clearance and oxygen delivery, which can manifest as a smaller rise in blood lactate during recovery (8).

Nevertheless, there is no strong evidence suggesting acute MTC supplementation reduces lactate in a sprint trial. Further indicated by Goa & Chilibeck meta-analysis stating that tart cherry products benefit recovery and markers of muscle damage, although effects on acute endurance performance are mixed and depend on dose, timing, and type of exercise (2).

Conclusion & Recommendations

In conclusion, acute MTC supplementation did not meaningfully change HR, completion time, or RPE, although a post-hoc paired comparison suggest a reduced post-exercise blood lactate with MTC. Future studies should attempt to control external motivation and alter the protocol to start supplementation earlier to increase polyphenol dosing to raise circulating levels before any sprint exercise, and track recovery to determine the optimum role of MTC dosing.
